    Learning goals

    By the end of this lesson, you will learn:

    1. What price is, why it matters, and the four approaches companies use to pick an approximate price level.
    2. What a demand curve shows and how revenue drives pricing decisions.
    3. How fixed and variable costs work together to set a break-even point.
    4. The objectives companies chase and the constraints that limit the prices they can charge.
    5. The steps a company takes to set a final price.

    Warm Up · Critical thinking

    The Water Bottle Challenge

    Facilitator note Have students set their prices silently, then poll the room for the highest and lowest at the stadium. Ask: "The bottle didn't change. What did?" Steer toward perceived benefits (convenience, urgency, lack of alternatives) and land on Value = Perceived benefits ÷ Price.

    It's the same 16.9 oz bottle of water in all four places. Slide to show the most you would pay in each situation.

    Value = Perceived benefits ÷ Price

    Price is the money (or other considerations) exchanged for owning or using a product. Value is what you get compared with what you pay. The water didn't change. Your thirst, your options, and your situation changed the benefits you saw, so your price changed too.

    You swap your old video game for a friend's hoodie. No money changes hands. Did you pay a price?

    The Pricing Toolkit

    To set an approximate price level, strategists look at four things: what buyers want (demand), what it costs (cost), what the company needs to earn (profit), and what rivals charge (competition). Pick a lens to explore its tools.

      Movement along vs. shift of the demand curve

      A demand curve shows the most units buyers will purchase at each price. Red Baron frozen cheese pizza starts at $8, selling 2 million a year. Demand depends on consumer tastes, the price and availability of similar products, and consumer income. Try both moves:

      Start point Q1: $8 per pizza, 2 million pizzas a year.

      Price elasticity of demand

      Elasticity = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

      Elastic

      Buyers react a lot. A 1% price cut brings more than a 1% jump in quantity. Often products with many substitutes.

      Inelastic

      Buyers barely react. A 1% price cut brings less than a 1% jump in quantity. Necessities tend to be inelastic.

        Break-even simulator: The Frame Shop

        The break-even point (BEP) is the quantity where total revenue equals total cost. BEP = Fixed cost ÷ (Price − Unit variable cost)

        Fixed costs stay the same no matter how much you sell. Variable costs (materials, direct labor, commissions) rise with every unit. TC = FC + VC TR = P × Q Profit = TR − TC

        Explore · Objectives, constraints, final price

        Rules of the Game

        Facilitator note Quick discussion: "Which objective would a new food truck pick? A 100-year-old company? A nonprofit clinic?" Stress that the four legal issues are illegal practices, not strategies. Students should be able to spot them in the news.

        Pricing objectives: what is the price supposed to accomplish?

        Pricing objectives spell out the role price plays in a company's marketing and strategic plans. Tap a card to flip it.

        Pricing constraints: what limits the price?

        Pricing constraints are factors that limit the range of prices a company can set.

        Demand

        For the product class (cars), product group (sports cars), and brand (Bugatti Chiron).

        Newness

        Stage in the product life cycle. Brand-new products are usually priced higher.

        Cost

        The cost of producing and marketing the product sets a floor over the long run.

        Competitors

        What rivals charge, and how they'll react if you move.

        Legal and ethical

        Laws protect buyers and competitors from unfair pricing. See below.

          Setting a final price in three steps

          The final price must be high enough to cover costs and meet objectives, but low enough that customers will buy.

          Select an approximate price level

          Use the demand, cost, profit, and competition tools from the Toolkit.

          Set the list or quoted price

          One-price policy: same price for everyone (CarMax's no-haggle prices). Dynamic pricing: prices flex by buyer and situation (Uber surge pricing).

          Make special adjustments

          Discounts (quantity, seasonal, trade, cash), allowances (trade-in, promotional), and everyday low pricing (EDLP) vs. high-low pricing.
